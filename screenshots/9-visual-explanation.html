Scaling restores softmax sensitivity

Dividing scores by √dₖ moves softmax away from saturation, making attention weights more responsive to score changes.

1 / 3

1. Two gaps, one softmax curve

Illustrative example
Read the explanation

For two keys, the first key’s probability depends only on the score gap Δ = s₁ − s₂: p = 1/(1 + exp(−Δ)). Both points lie on this same sigmoid. At A, increasing the gap barely changes p because it is already near 1. At B, a small gap change produces a larger probability change. The local slope is p(1 − p): approximately 0.000335 at A and 0.1966 at B. These are illustrative calculations, not measurements from the paper. The plotted curve is sampled; the labeled probabilities are rounded.

2. Divide by 8, then follow the gradient

Illustrative example
Read the explanation

The paper divides scores by √dₖ before softmax. Here √64 = 8, so the original gap g = 8 becomes the scaled gap Δ = g/8 = 1. Without scaling, dp/dg ≈ 0.000335. With scaling, the chain rule gives dp/dg = p(1 − p)/8 ≈ 0.1966/8 = 0.0246, about 73 times larger. Comparing only slopes on the sigmoid would give about 586 times, but that omits the factor 1/8 from scaling. These toy calculations illustrate the paper’s saturation concern. They describe the softmax contribution to a gradient; the full learning signal also depends on the loss and the rest of the model.

3. Why √dₖ, rather than dₖ?

Based on supplied text
Read the explanation

Footnote 4 assumes independent query and key components with mean 0 and variance 1. Each product qᵢkᵢ then has mean 0 and variance 1. Adding dₖ independent products gives Var(q · k) = dₖ, hence standard deviation √dₖ. Variance scales with the square of a multiplier: dividing the score by √dₖ therefore makes its variance 1. Dividing by dₖ instead would make its variance 1/dₖ, shrinking typical score differences toward zero as dimension grows and making attention more uniform. For dₖ = 64, the model predicts standard deviations 8 before scaling, 1 after dividing by 8, and 1/8 after dividing by 64. These are consequences of the footnote’s simplified assumptions, not guarantees about every learned query and key. Scaling controls typical spread; it does not guarantee that every softmax input avoids saturation.

Takeaways

  • Softmax becomes insensitive when score differences put its probabilities near 0 or 1.
  • In this toy example, scaling increases sensitivity to the original gap about 73-fold, including the chain-rule factor.
  • √dₖ normalizes the footnote’s predicted standard deviation; dₖ would overcompress typical score differences.
